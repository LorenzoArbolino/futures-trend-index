# Futures trend index

A systematic trend-following index across commodity, government-bond and currency futures,
designed to be held **on top of an equity portfolio** as a futures overlay. It is built
end-to-end from free public data in pure Python (standard library only) and backtested from
1989 to 2026 with realistic costs.

## The strategy

Once a month, at month-end:

1. **Universe.** Every January the tradable set is re-selected from 40 liquid futures using
   only data available at the time. In order of liquidity, a market joins unless one already
   held explains about half its variance (monthly-return correlation ≥ 0.70); incumbents get a
   small margin to avoid churn. Performance is never an input. Between 19 and 26 markets
   have qualified over time.
2. **Signal.** The direction score is the average sign of the 1-, 3- and 12-month excess
   returns (Hurst, Ooi & Pedersen, 2017), blended 75/25 with carry, i.e. what the position
   earns if prices do not move (rate differentials, term spreads, futures-curve roll yield;
   Koijen et al., 2018).
3. **Sizing.** Risk is split equally across sectors, then across economic clusters within a
   sector (energy, metals, grains, softs; regional bond and currency blocs), then across the
   markets in each cluster. Each market's volatility estimate (36 months) is floored at half
   its sector's median.
4. **Risk.** The book is scaled toward 15% ex-ante annual volatility from the trailing
   covariance of the markets held, with gross notional capped at 8× capital.
5. **Costs.** About one tick per trade, plus contract rolls; tripled before 1995 and doubled
   for 1995–2004.

## Why it is built this way

- **Where the returns come from.** Trend following is paid for persistent price moves:
  information diffuses slowly, and hedgers and constrained investors keep trading in the
  direction of the move. These moves occur in many unrelated markets and at different speeds,
  so the robust lever is **breadth, not prediction**. The design concentrates on spreading
  risk evenly across genuinely different bets and horizons.
- **Markets move together more since 2008.** Cross-asset correlation rose after 2008, driven
  by risk-on/risk-off moves and policy-driven common trends. Forty candidate markets behave
  like only about 6–11 independent bets. Cluster risk budgets stop a group of similar markets
  from taking a full share each, and the correlation-aware volatility target sizes the book to
  its actual diversification.
- **Central banks suppressed volatility for long stretches.** Zero rates and yield-curve
  control made some bonds look almost riskless, so risk parity would lever them many times
  over. The sector volatility floor limits this, because suppressed volatility tends to end
  abruptly (the Swiss franc floor in 2015, the end of yield-curve control).
- **Sharper reversals.** Policy responses turned several trends around quickly. Blending
  1-, 3- and 12-month horizons reacts faster than a single slow lookback without chasing noise.
- **Markets evolve.** Liquidity migrates and contracts appear, so the universe is reviewed
  every year by fixed, outcome-blind rules, as a committee-run index would do.

None of this is market timing: the rules are identical in every period.

## Key findings

1. **Free data can fake a track record.** Several free datasets publish *monthly averages*.
   On pure random walks, a 25-market trend index shows a Sharpe ratio of about 1.4 on monthly
   averages and about 0 on month-end prices. Averaged returns are autocorrelated by
   construction (Working, 1960). An earlier version of this project was inflated in exactly
   this way.
2. **Most design choices are defensible priors, not proven improvements.** Paired bootstrap
   intervals show only a few choices clearly beating zero on their own. The case for the rest
   is prior reasoning and a consistent direction.
3. **No evidence that markets that trended badly keep doing so.** Past and future trend
   Sharpe ratios are essentially uncorrelated across markets. Pruning markets by backtest is
   curve-fitting.
4. **Why trend following weakened after 2008.** Portfolio Sharpe ≈ average single-market
   Sharpe × a diversification multiplier. About 80% of the post-2008 decline is weaker trends
   in the average market; about 20% is lost diversification (effective bets roughly halved).
5. **Stacking works as intended.** The overlay gained on average in the months equities fell
   most. On top of the S&P 500 it raised the combined Sharpe ratio and reduced the worst
   drawdown.
6. **Weekly rebalancing** (tested on daily commodity data) mainly improves risk: a shallower
   drawdown and steadier volatility, at twice the turnover. Executing a day late costs a
   little.

## Validation and limitations

- **Pseudo out-of-sample.** The design was refined on the full sample. The period from 2008
  is reported separately but is not a clean holdout, and results from 2008 are materially
  weaker than before.
- **Concentration.** Japan (yen and JGBs) contributed about a third of the profit; without it
  the Sharpe ratio is noticeably lower. Decades of falling Japanese yields may not repeat.
- **Data.**
  - Commodities use daily back-adjusted futures to March 2024. After that they use Yahoo
    front-month prices mapped onto the futures by regression (holdout tracking error 4–24% a
    year by market).
  - Bonds are built from yields with a duration model, and currencies from spot plus the
    rate differential. Both correlate 0.93–0.98 with real futures (`research.md` §8).
  - Liquidity ranks use today's volumes.
- **Implementation.**
  - Monthly rebalancing at the signal close.
  - Estimated exchange margin is typically about 15% of capital, peaking near 30%.
  - Results are before fees. A flat management fee of 1% or 2% a year lowers the Sharpe ratio
    to about 0.82 or 0.76.

## Reproduce

Requires Python 3.10+, no packages.

```bash
python run.py all                    # fetch -> sanity -> backtest -> report -> research
python -m unittest discover -s tests # 58 tests; the end-to-end test builds a synthetic database
```

`python run.py fetch` caches raw downloads in `data/cache/`. If the Bundesbank API is
unavailable, the error message lists the three files to download manually.

```
config.py           every assumption: universe, selection rule, signal, sizing, costs
run.py              command line: fetch, sanity, backtest, report, research
mf/sources.py       download and parse each source (and what its monthly value means)
mf/panel.py         data assembly, commodity splice, universe selection, carry, variants
mf/model.py         signals, risk budgets, volatility targeting, costs, backtest loop
mf/research.py      the research studies behind output/research.md
tests/              look-ahead, timing, reconciliation, data contracts, end-to-end
```
